The before

Be honest — how many tabs is your business running on right now?

You know this version of the day. The DMs pile up while you're mid-appointment, so you answer them at 11pm. Bookings live in one app, payments in another, your client list in a notes file, and the real source of truth is somewhere in your own head. You re-type the same order details three times because nothing talks to anything. You meant to follow up with the client who ghosted in March, but March was four hundred messages ago.

None of it is broken, exactly. That's the trap. The lash studio still runs. The boutique still ships. The bakery still gets the cake out the door. But it runs on you — on your memory, your late nights, your willingness to be the glue holding twelve fragile little systems together. Growth doesn't feel like opportunity anymore. It feels like more of the thing that's already exhausting you.

Meanwhile the tools that could fix all of this exist. Automations, custom apps, an assistant that answers at 2am, systems that remember every client so you don't have to. They've been quietly running billion-dollar companies for years. The problem was never the technology — it was that nobody built a bridge from that world to yours, and made it actually fit a business your size. That bridge is the whole reason I'm here, and to explain it I have to back up a little.

It's not that your business is broken. It's that it only works because you never stop.
Where it starts

A New York kid, a machine that obeyed, and two degrees on purpose

I grew up in New York, and I fell for technology early — the way some kids fall for a sport. There was something about a computer that hooked me before I had the words for it: you typed an instruction, and the machine did precisely what you said. Not what it felt like, not what it remembered from yesterday — exactly what you told it. To a curious kid, that felt like a kind of magic you could actually learn. So I learned it. I took the thing apart, broke it, fixed it, broke it again, and slowly stopped being the kid who used computers and became the kid who built things with them.

When it came time for college, I made a choice that confused a few people. I didn't just study Computer Science — I went and got a second degree in Business Management. People asked why I'd double the work. The honest answer is that I never believed software was the point. I believed it was the tool. The point was always the business it served, the owner whose life it changed, the money it saved or made.

Engineers who can't read a P&L tend to build beautiful things nobody asked for. Business folks who can't read code tend to get talked into expensive things they don't need. I wanted to be neither. So I learned both languages on purpose — and I've spent my career as a translator between them, the person in the room who can hear a business problem and answer it in working software. What that means for you is simple: you don't have to learn to speak engineer, and you don't have to hope your idea survives translation. You tell me what's slowing your business down in your own words. I turn it into a system. Then I hand it back to you in language that still makes sense — what it does, what it cost, and what it returns. No jargon used as a smokescreen. No "trust me, it's technical."

You tell me what's slowing you down in plain English. I turn it into a working system — and explain it back in plain English.
The Fortune 500 years

A decade building the tools big companies take for granted — then leaving to share them

For more than ten years, I built software inside Fortune 500 companies. Enterprise-grade systems — the automations, the integrations, the custom internal apps, the quiet machinery that runs in the background and makes a giant company feel effortless to its customers. I learned how the big players actually operate. Not the buzzwords — the mechanics. How they wire their systems together so data flows instead of getting re-typed by hand. How they automate the repetitive work so their people can do the work that matters. None of it is mysterious once you've built it. It's just expensive, and it's been locked behind a door most businesses never get to open.

Day after day I'd ship something powerful for a company with thousands of employees, and the same thought kept tapping me on the shoulder: the corner bakery could use a version of this. So could the lash tech booked solid and drowning in DMs. They'd benefit from it more than the giant ever would — they just had no way in. Those tools were never actually impossible for a small business. They were just out of reach: too costly, too complicated, wrapped in jargon, and sold by firms with no interest in a client who isn't a household name.

So small-business owners did what they've always done — they white-knuckled it. They ran the whole operation out of their own head and their phone, answered every message at midnight, re-typed the same information into five different places, and lost real money to busywork they never should have been doing. Not because they weren't smart or hungry enough — they're the hardest workers I know — but because nobody had ever handed them the keys. I couldn't unsee it. I'd built those keys with my own hands for a decade. So I started MakeThisQuick on a simple promise: you get the same caliber of engineering a Fortune 500 gets — built by the same hands, held to the same standard — at a scale that fits your business. Not a watered-down "small business edition." The actual craft, finally pointed at the people who need it most.

I couldn't unsee it. I'd built those keys with my own hands for a decade.
What changed everything

Then AI arrived — and the door came off the hinges

For most of my career, the gap between what an enterprise could afford and what a small business could afford was just brutal economics. Building custom software took time, and time took money, and that math quietly kept the little guys out. AI changed that math almost overnight. The technology got accessible. The translation didn't.

That second part is everything. A solo lash tech doesn't have a CTO to tell her which AI assistant is worth her time. A corner boutique owner can't take three weeks off the floor to map her workflows and wire up an automation. So the same founders who could benefit most from this stuff are the ones standing outside, watching the advantage go to companies that already had every advantage. That strikes me as genuinely unfair — and it's worth moving on now, while the edge is still there for the taking, before it quietly becomes table stakes and the shops who waited spend years catching up.

Here's the part the hype always skips: the tools in your pocket are worthless if nobody sets them up to actually serve your business. That's the real work now, and it's exactly where I come in. I make it work, I make it right, and I make it intelligent — so your business doesn't just own powerful tools, it actually uses them. That's why I call MakeThisQuick an AI digital-transformation studio rather than a software shop. The AI is the engine. Knowing what your business actually needs, and having the engineering to build it properly, is the part you can't fake.

The technology got accessible. The translation didn't. That gap is the whole reason this studio exists.
